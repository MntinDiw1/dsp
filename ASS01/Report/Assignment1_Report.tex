\section{Recordings}
% =====================================================
Will spoke one English sentence, ``\textit{Saad and Will are electrowizards}'', recorded at \SI{5}{\centi\metre} and \SI{1}{\metre} as uncompressed mono WAV files (\SI{48}{\kilo\hertz}, 24-bit).
The full spectrum up to \SI{20}{\kilo\hertz} (the human audible spectrum) is available, and neither recording is clipped (peaks at \SI{-22.5}{\decibel FS} and \SI{-44.5}{\decibel FS}).

\section{Task 1: Time and Frequency Analysis}
% =====================================================
The samples are normalised by $2^{31}$, the full scale of the 32-bit signed integers used to store the 24-bit audio.
The spectrum is $X = \frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{FFT}(x)$, plotted as $20\log_{10}|X|$ in decibels relative to full scale (dBFS): the loudest level possible in a digital system is \SI{0}{\decibel FS}.
Only the positive half is shown, because the spectrum of a real signal is mirrored.

\paragraph{Fundamentals.}
In both recordings the vowels appear as series of evenly spaced peaks.
The lowest peak of each series was taken as the fundamental, because the peaks above it sit at integer multiples of it (e.g.\ \SI{95}{}, \SI{190}{}, \SI{285}{} and \SI{380}{\hertz} at \SI{5}{\centi\metre}), which identifies a harmonic series rather than noise.
The fundamentals lie between about \SI{94}{} and \SI{103}{\hertz} at \SI{5}{\centi\metre} and \SI{101}{} and \SI{112}{\hertz} at \SI{1}{\metre}, as expected for an adult male voice.
They vary between recordings because these are separate takes.
Peaks below about \SI{80}{\hertz} have no harmonic series and were not chosen; these are low-frequency rumble from pop energy and room noise.

\section{Task 2: Speech Enhancement}
% =====================================================

\paragraph{Method.}
Each recording is transformed with the FFT, then every frequency bin is multiplied by a gain array (an array of ones), and the result is transformed back with the IFFT.
\texttt{gain\_ctrl(low, high, dB, q)} adds one band to the array: a trapezoid over the selected frequencies, converted to linear gain with $10^{\mathrm{dB}/20}$ and mirrored onto the negative frequencies so that the IFFT output is real (\texttt{np.real} only removes rounding residue).
Hard band edges caused audible ringing (Gibbs phenomenon), so each edge ramps over a fraction $q$ of the band width, with the nominal edge at the middle of the ramp.
An identity test (gain array at default values) confirmed that the pipeline alone does not change the signal at all.
The outputs are scaled by $2^{31}-1$ and saved as 32-bit integer WAV.

\paragraph{5\,cm.}
A low cut of \SI{-16}{\decibel} below \SI{80}{\hertz} removes rumble and part of the pop energy (the pop at \SIrange{1.28}{1.30}{\second} lies mostly below \SI{150}{\hertz}).
It stops at \SI{80}{\hertz} to keep the $\approx$\SI{95}{\hertz} fundamental, so the pop is slightly reduced but not removed.
The low end is already strong at this proximity, so the bass boost is small: \SI{+4}{\decibel} between \SIrange{95}{400}{\hertz}; this range covers the first few harmonics for depth.
The consonant region (\SIrange{3}{10}{\kilo\hertz}) is boosted by \SI{+6}{\decibel} for clarity and `brightness'.
A high cut of \SI{-12}{\decibel} above \SI{12.2}{\kilo\hertz} reduces hiss.
It is kept moderate because consonant energy extends to \SI{14.5}{\kilo\hertz} at this distance.

\paragraph{1\,m.}
The whole spectrum is raised by \SI{+21}{\decibel} to match the volume of both recordings.
A low cut of \SI{-12}{\decibel} below \SI{85}{\hertz} removes room rumble.
Bass is lost with distance, so the gain is larger to compensate: \SI{+6}{\decibel} between \SIrange{95}{180}{\hertz}, around the fundamental.

\section{Task 3: Aural Exciter}
% =====================================================

\paragraph{Design.}
The exciter follows the Aphex sidechain (Figure~\ref{fig:block}), ignoring the Big Bottom sidechain, and runs on the raw \SI{5}{\centi\metre} recording.
A band-pass filter is created by zeroing all FFT bins outside a specified region (two \texttt{gain\_ctrl} cuts).
A band-pass was used instead of the tunable high-pass, so that less of the voice enters the non-linearity and fewer unwanted harmonics are created.
The band is normalised to a peak of 1, so that the drive does not depend on the recording level, and passed through $\tanh(\mathrm{drive}\cdot x)$.
This is almost linear for small inputs and compresses progressively as they grow.
Because $\tanh$ is an odd function, it creates only odd-order harmonics.
A second band-pass keeps the wanted products further up the spectrum, which are scaled by the mix value and added to the dry signal.
The pop was not removed before processing; however, since it lies mostly below the sidechain band, the pop is barely amplified.

\paragraph{Iterations.}
The versions are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:iter}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Exciter versions (dry recording: peak 0.0753, RMS 0.00822).}
  \label{tab:iter}
  \begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
    \toprule
    Version & Sidechain (Hz) & Keep band (Hz) & Drive & Mix & Output peak & RMS \\
    \midrule
    V1         & 1750--2250 & 5000--7000  & 10 & 0.5  & 0.242 & 0.02523 \\
    V2         & 1750--2250 & 5000--7000  & 3  & 0.1  & 0.075 & 0.00830 \\
    V3         & 2000--4000 & 4000--12000 & 3  & 0.25 & 0.130 & 0.00835 \\
    V4         & 2000--4000 & 5500--12000 & 6  & 0.15 & 0.099 & 0.00857 \\
    V5 (final) & 2000--4000 & 5500--10000 & 4  & 0.25 & 0.116 & 0.00845 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{description}
  \item[V1:] sidechain \SIrange{1750}{2250}{\hertz}, centred on \SI{2}{\kilo\hertz} so that its 3rd harmonic lands at \SI{6}{\kilo\hertz}, where the ear is most sensitive; keep band \SIrange{5}{7}{\kilo\hertz}; drive 10, mix 0.5.
  The plot confirms that only odd harmonics appear (\SI{6}{}, \SI{10}{} and \SI{14}{\kilo\hertz}), but the voice sounds robotic and metallic, and the output peak is very large, so the drive and mix were reduced.
  \item[V2:] drive 3, mix 0.1.
  The peak is unchanged and the ``k'' and click sounds are clearer, but the effect is confined to one narrow region, so the bands were widened.
  \item[V3:] sidechain \SIrange{2}{4}{\kilo\hertz} (3rd harmonics now span \SIrange{6}{12}{\kilo\hertz}), keep band \SIrange{4}{12}{\kilo\hertz}, mix 0.25.
  The effect is broader but rings, and the peak rises to 1.7 times the dry peak.
  \item[V4:] the keep band starts at \SI{5.5}{\kilo\hertz}, just below the lowest 3rd harmonic, and ends at \SI{12}{\kilo\hertz}; mix 0.15; drive raised in steps to 6.
  The effect is clearly audible, but the consonants become too prominent (a lisp effect).
  \item[V5 (final):] drive back to 4, keep band ending at \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz} to remove the content behind the lisp, and the mix tuned by ear to 0.25.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Final design.}
Sidechain \SIrange{2}{4}{\kilo\hertz}, drive 4, keep band \SIrange{5.5}{10}{\kilo\hertz}, mix 0.25.
The keep band holds the 3rd harmonics of \SIrange{2}{3.3}{\kilo\hertz} and excludes both the sidechain band itself and the content above \SI{10}{\kilo\hertz}.
The output volume is roughly the same RMS and the peak rises by \SI{3.7}{\decibel}.
With $\mathrm{RMS} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_n x[n]^2}$ over the whole recording, the level changes are
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta L_\mathrm{RMS} = 20\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{RMS}_\mathrm{out}}{\mathrm{RMS}_\mathrm{dry}} = 20\log_{10}\frac{0.00845}{0.00822} = \SI{+0.24}{\decibel},
  \qquad
  \Delta L_\mathrm{peak} = 20\log_{10}\frac{0.1157}{0.0753} = \SI{+3.73}{\decibel}.
\end{equation*}
The k and s sounds are more pronounced and the voice sounds closer, while its level barely changes, which is the intended effect of an exciter.
