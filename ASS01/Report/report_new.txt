1. Recordings

Will spoke one English sentence, "Saad and Will are electrowizards", recorded at 5 cm and 1 m as uncompressed mono WAV files (48 kHz, 24-bit). The full spectrum up to 20 kHz (human audible spectrum) is available, and neither recording is clipped (peaks at -22.5 and -44.5 dB).

2. Task 1: Time and frequency analysis

The samples are normalised by 2³¹, the full scale of the 32-bit signed integers used to store the 24-bit audio. The spectrum is X = 1/N * FFT(x), plotted as 20·log₁₀|X|, decibels relative to full scale (dBFS): the loudest level possible in a digital system is 0. Only the positive half is shown, because the spectrum of a real signal is mirrored.

[Figure 1: 5 cm, time and frequency] [Figure 2: 1 m, time and frequency]

Fundamentals. In both recordings the vowels appear as series of evenly spaced peaks. The lowest peak of each series was taken as the fundamental, because the peaks above it sit at integer multiples of it (e.g. 95, 190, 285 and 380 Hz at 5 cm), which identifies a harmonic series rather than noise. The fundamentals lie between about 94 and 103 Hz at 5 cm and 101 and 112 Hz at 1 m, as expected for an adult male voice. They vary between recordings because these are separate takes. Peaks below about 80 Hz have no harmonic series and were not chosen, these are low-frequency rumble from pop energy and room noise. [MENTION THE PEAKS OF OTHER VOWELS?]

Consonants. Above about 2 kHz the harmonic series fades and a broad hump without harmonic structure appears, peaking at 5–6 kHz: the energy of consonants such as 's' and 'z'. It is present on the frequency spectrum up to about 14.5 kHz at 5 cm but only 12.5 kHz at 1 m, where the weaker consonant energy is lost in the noise floor sooner. [THERE IS A GAP BETWEEN THE NOISE AND THE CONSONANTS!!!]

Noise. Above 16 kHz at 5 cm and 14 kHz at 1 m, the spectrum is flat. The level is the same in both recordings even though the voice is about 20 dB quieter at 1 m, so it cannot come from the voice: thus it is mostlikely electronic noise.

3. Task 2: Speech enhancement

Method. Each recording is transformed with the FFT, then every frequency bin is multiplied by a gain array (an array of ones), and the result is transformed back with the IFFT. gain_ctrl(low, high, dB, q) adds one 'band' to the array: a trapezoid over the selected frequencies, converts it to linear gain with 10^(dB/20), and mirrors it onto the negative frequencies so that the IFFT output is real (np.real only removes rounding residue). Hard band edges caused audible ringing (Gibbs phenomenon), so each edge ramps over a fraction q of the band width, with the nominal edge at the middle of the ramp. An identity test (gain array at default values) confirmed that the pipeline alone doesnt change the signal at all. The outputs are scaled by 2³¹ − 1 and saved as 32-bit integer WAV.

[Table 1: final gain settings] [Figure: gain curves] [Figure: enhanced spectra overlay]

5 cm. A low cut of -16 dB below 80 Hz removes rumble and part of the pop energy (the pop at 1.28–1.30 s lies mostly below 150 Hz). It stops at 80 Hz to keep the ≈95 Hz fundamental, so the pop is slightly reduced but not removed. The low end is already strong at this proximity, so the bass boost is small: +4 dB between 95–400 Hz, the this range covers the first few harmonics for depth. The consonant region (3–10 kHz) is boosted by +6 dB for clarity and 'brightness'. A high cut of -12 dB above 12.2 kHz reduces hiss. It is kept moderate because consonant energy extends to 14.5 kHz at this distance. [Make into a table?]

1 m. The whole spectrum is raised by +21 dB to match the volume of both recordings. A low cut of -12 dB below 85 Hz removes room rumble. Bass is lost with distance, so the gain is larger to compensate: +6 dB between 95–180 Hz, around the fundamental. The low cut and bass boost edges meet at 85–95 Hz and initially rang with q = 0.1, so both use q = 0.3 which eliminated any high-frequency artifacts. A -12 dB notch at 480–505 Hz removes a 500 Hz tone visible in the noise floor after the voice ends. A -4 dB cut at 2.2–2.8 kHz reduces room resonance/reverb. The consonant region is boosted by +7 dB, slightly more than at 5 cm, because the distant recording is duller. A -30 dB high cut above 13 kHz, just above where the consonants end, removes the hiss raised by the level match. There was no pop at this distance, however since the volume was increased so much, the SNR was reduced to the point that the signal sounds noisy. [Tabulate?]
